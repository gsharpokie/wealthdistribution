Who Has the Wealth?

Imagine the United States as 100 households, lined up from least wealthy to most, sharing $100 of wealth. Wealth means everything a household owns (home, savings, retirement accounts, stocks) minus what it owes. Divide the $100 the way you think it’s really divided.

The fuller picture

Most people underestimate the gap. In a 2005 survey of 5,522 Americans, people guessed that the richest fifth of households held about 59% of the country’s wealth. At the time it held about 84%. Asked what would be ideal, they said about 32%. Republicans and Democrats, and richer and poorer people, described similar ideals.

The top share is at a record. The top 1% held 22.8% of wealth when the Federal Reserve’s series began in 1989 and 32.5% in mid-2026, the highest in the series.

The bottom half has recovered from its low point, though not to where it started. Its share fell from 3.5% in 1989 to 0.4% after the housing crash (2010–2012). It was 2.3% in mid-2026.

Wealth isn’t income, and age matters. People usually build wealth over a lifetime, so a 25-year-old with student loans and a 65-year-old with a paid-off house can land in very different groups without being in different social classes. These figures also don’t count future Social Security benefits, which matter most for households with little other wealth.

Talk about it

  1. How close was your guess? Which group did you miss by the most, and why do you think that is?
  2. Most of us rarely see how much wealth other people have. What do we see instead, and how might that shape our guesses?
  3. In the 2005 survey, people across political lines said they’d prefer a more equal split than the real one. If that’s so, why don’t more people push for it? Economists and political scientists point to beliefs about mobility, disagreement about causes, and not knowing the real numbers. Which seems most convincing?
  4. Would the picture look different if we measured income instead of wealth? Why?
  5. What would you want to know about the households in the bottom 50 before deciding how much of a problem the gap is? Think about age, debt, and home ownership.

About the data

Shares of household net worth come from the Federal Reserve’s Distributional Financial Accounts for the second quarter of 2026 (released September 18, 2026): bottom 50% of households, 2.3%; next 40%, 28.8%; next 9%, 36.4%; top 1%, 32.5%. The Fed updates these every quarter and revises earlier quarters. The 100-household picture is these shares applied to $100.

Sources: Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System, Distributional Financial Accounts, via FRED (series WFRBST01134 and WFRBSB50215). Norton, Michael I. and Dan Ariely. 2011. “Building a Better America—One Wealth Quintile at a Time.” Perspectives on Psychological Science 6(1): 9–12.
